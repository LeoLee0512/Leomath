## Start from a problem

<Problem>
$y'=f(t,y)$ assigns a slope to every point of the plane. How do we recover the whole curve from "slopes everywhere"?

The previous section proved that a solution exists and is unique, but not how to compute it. We need methods that actually produce solutions, even if only for certain types of equation.
</Problem>

<Observe>
Choose "Logistic growth" below: $y'=ry\left(1-\dfrac yK\right)$. Vary the initial value $y(0)$: solutions starting below climb, solutions starting above fall, all towards $K$; the one starting at $y(0)=0$ stays $0$ forever. No two solution curves ever cross.

<Experiment slug="ode-explorer" preset="logistic" />
</Observe>

<Conjecture>
A first-order equation $y'=f(t,y)$ prescribes a slope at every point $(t,y)$. Solution curves are the curves tangent to the prescribed slope everywhere. By uniqueness (see [What is an ODE](/en/concepts/what-is-ode)) exactly one solution passes through each point, so they cannot cross. And if $f$ factors as a function of $t$ times a function of $y$, perhaps we can move $y$ and $t$ to opposite sides and [integrate](/en/concepts/integral) each.
</Conjecture>

## Separation of variables

<Proposition title="Separable equations">
If $y'=g(t)h(y)$ with $h(y)\ne0$, solutions satisfy
$$\int\frac{dy}{h(y)}=\int g(t)\,dt.$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Let $H$ be an antiderivative of $\dfrac1h$ and $G$ one of $g$. By the chain rule
$$\frac{d}{dt}H(y(t))=H'(y)\,y'=\frac{y'}{h(y)}=g(t)=G'(t),$$
so $H(y(t))=G(t)+C$.
</Proof>

Writing $\dfrac{dy}{h(y)}=g(t)\,dt$ is a mnemonic; what actually happens is the chain rule run backwards.

<Example title="y′ = y²">
$\int y^{-2}\,dy=\int dt\Rightarrow-\dfrac1y=t+C$. From $y(0)=1$, $C=-1$ and $y=\dfrac1{1-t}$. The solution blows up as $t\to1^-$: first-order solutions can reach infinity in finite time.
</Example>

<Example title="The logistic equation">
$\displaystyle\int\frac{dy}{y(1-y/K)}=\int r\,dt$. Partial fractions, $\dfrac1{y(1-y/K)}=\dfrac1y+\dfrac{1/K}{1-y/K}$, give $\ln|y|-\ln|1-y/K|=rt+C$, hence
$$y(t)=\frac{K}{1+\left(\frac K{y_0}-1\right)e^{-rt}}.$$
As $t\to\infty$, $y\to K$, matching the experiment. For $y_0=0$ separation is illegal ($h(0)=0$), but $y\equiv0$ is obviously a solution, an **equilibrium**; so is $y\equiv K$.
</Example>

## Linear equations and integrating factors

<Definition title="First-order linear equation">
$$y'+p(t)y=q(t).$$
</Definition>

Separation fails: the right side is not $g(t)h(y)$. But the left side is "almost" the derivative of a product.

<Theorem title="Integrating factor">
Let $\mu(t)=e^{\int p(t)\,dt}$. Then $y'+py=q$ is equivalent to $(\mu y)'=\mu q$, so
$$y=\frac1{\mu(t)}\left(\int\mu(t)q(t)\,dt+C\right).$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$\mu'=p\mu$; that is the entire reason for the choice of $\mu$ (see $(e^u)'=u'e^u$ in [The derivative](/en/concepts/derivative)). Hence
$$(\mu y)'=\mu y'+\mu'y=\mu y'+p\mu y=\mu(y'+py)=\mu q.$$
Integrate and divide by $\mu$.
</Proof>

<Example>
$y'+2y=e^t$: $\mu=e^{2t}$, $(e^{2t}y)'=e^{3t}$, $e^{2t}y=\tfrac13e^{3t}+C$, $y=\tfrac13e^t+Ce^{-2t}$.
</Example>

<Theorem title="Structure of solutions of a linear equation">
The general solution of $y'+py=q$ equals the general solution $Ce^{-\int p}$ of the homogeneous equation $y'+py=0$ plus any one particular solution.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
If $y_1,y_2$ both solve $y'+py=q$, then $(y_1-y_2)'+p(y_1-y_2)=q-q=0$, so $y_1-y_2$ is a homogeneous solution. Conversely, homogeneous solution plus particular solution is again a solution.
</Proof>

This structure comes from **linearity**: the solution set is an affine space, a translate of the homogeneous solution space, which is a one-dimensional [vector space](/en/concepts/vectors). The same structure reappears in [Second-order linear equations](/en/concepts/second-order-linear-ode), where that space becomes two-dimensional.

<Warning>
Dividing by $h(y)$ when separating variables throws away the **equilibrium solutions** where $h(y)=0$. For the logistic equation, $y\equiv0$ and $y\equiv K$ are lost this way and must be added back by hand. Every time you divide by an expression in $y$, ask what happens when it is zero.
</Warning>

## Application

<Application title="Geometry of solutions: the direction field">
Without solving, draw at each point a short segment of slope $f(t,y)$: the direction field. Solution curves are the curves that follow it. The short segments in the experiment's phase portrait are a direction field (for a two-dimensional system). Equilibria are horizontal lines where $f(t,y)=0$; for the logistic equation $y=K$ attracts nearby solutions and $y=0$ repels them.
</Application>

<Remark>
Only a few classes of first-order equations have closed-form solutions: separable, linear, exact, and some reducible by substitution. Most cannot be solved in closed form, which does not stop us analysing them (direction fields, equilibria, stability) or [solving them numerically](/en/concepts/numerical-ode).
</Remark>
