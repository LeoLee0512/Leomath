## Start from a problem

<Problem>
In the home-page experiment you dragged only two arrows, yet infinitely many points of the plane moved with them. What gives two vectors control over every point?

To answer that we must first pin down which transformations "respect" vector addition and scaling. Those should be completely determined by what they do to a basis.
</Problem>

<Observe>
Drag the tips of the two basis vectors $\mathbf e_1$, $\mathbf e_2$ below. **The whole grid** follows: straight lines stay straight, parallel lines stay parallel, the origin stays put, equally spaced grid lines stay equally spaced.

You moved two points and thereby decided where every point of the plane goes. Why?

<Experiment slug="linear-transform" />
</Observe>

<Conjecture>
Because every vector in the plane is $v=x\mathbf e_1+y\mathbf e_2$, and if a transformation $T$ "respects" addition and scaling it must send $v$ to $xT(\mathbf e_1)+yT(\mathbf e_2)$. What $T$ does to a basis determines everything.
</Conjecture>

## Definition

<Definition title="Linear map">
Let $V,W$ be vector spaces. A map $T:V\to W$ is **linear** if for all $u,v\in V$ and $c\in\mathbb R$,
$$T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v),\qquad T(cv)=cT(v).$$
</Definition>

Together these say $T(au+bv)=aT(u)+bT(v)$: linear maps preserve linear combinations.

Note $T(0)=T(0\cdot0)=0\cdot T(0)=0$: a linear map always sends the origin to the origin. So a translation $v\mapsto v+b$ ($b\ne0$) is **not** linear, which is why the origin never moves in the experiment.

## Theorems and proofs

<Theorem title="A linear map is determined by its values on a basis">
Let $v_1,\dots,v_n$ be a basis of $V$ and $w_1,\dots,w_n\in W$ arbitrary. There is exactly one linear map $T:V\to W$ with $T(v_i)=w_i$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
**Existence.** Each $v\in V$ has unique coordinates $v=\sum c_iv_i$ (see [Vectors](/en/concepts/vectors)). Define $T(v)=\sum c_iw_i$. If $u=\sum d_iv_i$ then $au+bv=\sum(ad_i+bc_i)v_i$, so $T(au+bv)=\sum(ad_i+bc_i)w_i=aT(u)+bT(v)$. Thus $T$ is linear, and clearly $T(v_i)=w_i$.

**Uniqueness.** If $S$ is linear with $S(v_i)=w_i$, then $S(v)=S(\sum c_iv_i)=\sum c_iS(v_i)=\sum c_iw_i=T(v)$.
</Proof>

This is the theorem behind the experiment: two arrows, $T(\mathbf e_1)$ and $T(\mathbf e_2)$, decide the fate of the whole plane. A linear map of $n$-dimensional space is decided by $n$ vectors.

<Theorem title="Linear maps send lines to lines (or points)">
If $T$ is linear, the image of the line $\{p+tv:t\in\mathbb R\}$ is $\{T(p)+tT(v)\}$: a line when $T(v)\ne0$, otherwise a point. Parallel lines map to parallel lines (or degenerate together).
</Theorem>

<Proof>
$T(p+tv)=T(p)+tT(v)$ by linearity. Lines sharing the direction $v$ have images sharing the direction $T(v)$.
</Proof>

This explains "lines stay lines, parallels stay parallel". The "Collapse" preset, where everything is squashed onto one line, is the case where $T(\mathbf e_1)$ and $T(\mathbf e_2)$ are collinear and $T(v)=0$ has nonzero solutions.

<Definition title="Kernel and image">
$\ker T=\{v\in V:T(v)=0\}$ is the **kernel** of $T$; $\operatorname{im}T=\{T(v):v\in V\}$ is its **image**. Both are subspaces.
</Definition>

<Theorem title="Rank–nullity">
If $V$ is finite-dimensional, $\dim\ker T+\dim\operatorname{im}T=\dim V$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Take a basis $u_1,\dots,u_k$ of $\ker T$ and extend it to a basis $u_1,\dots,u_k,v_1,\dots,v_r$ of $V$. We claim $T(v_1),\dots,T(v_r)$ is a basis of $\operatorname{im}T$.

Spanning: any $T(v)$ with $v=\sum a_iu_i+\sum b_jv_j$ equals $\sum b_jT(v_j)$.

Independence: if $\sum b_jT(v_j)=0$ then $T(\sum b_jv_j)=0$, so $\sum b_jv_j\in\ker T$ and equals some $\sum a_iu_i$. Independence of the full basis forces all $b_j=0$.

Hence $\dim\operatorname{im}T=r=\dim V-k$.
</Proof>

In the experiment: when $T(\mathbf e_1),T(\mathbf e_2)$ are not collinear the kernel is $\{0\}$ and the image is the whole plane ($0+2=2$); when they are collinear and nonzero, kernel and image are each a line ($1+1=2$).

<Warning>
A linear map always sends $0$ to $0$. So a translation $v\mapsto v+b$ with $b\ne0$ is not linear, and the school "linear function" $y=kx+b$ is not a linear map when $b\ne0$ (it is affine). When testing linearity, check $T(0)$ first.
</Warning>

## Applications

<Application title="Rotation, projection and differentiation are linear">
- Rotation of the plane by $\theta$ about the origin: linear, $\mathbf e_1\mapsto(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$, $\mathbf e_2\mapsto(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$.
- Projection onto the $x$-axis: linear, kernel the $y$-axis.
- Differentiation $D:p\mapsto p'$ on polynomials: linear, $D(af+bg)=af'+bg'$, kernel the constants.

The last example is why linear algebra reappears in [Second-order linear equations](/en/concepts/second-order-linear-ode): solving a linear differential equation is finding the kernel of a linear map.
</Application>

<Remark>
The next section, [Matrices](/en/concepts/matrices), does one thing only: it writes the coordinates of $T(\mathbf e_1),T(\mathbf e_2)$ as two columns. That is a matrix. A matrix is a notation for a linear map, nothing more.
</Remark>
