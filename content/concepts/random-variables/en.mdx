## Start from a problem

<Problem>
A call centre has $1000$ customers. Each of them calls within a given hour with probability $0.3\%$, independently of the others. How many calls arrive in that hour? The number is random, of course; the real question is what the probability of $0,1,2,\dots$ calls **is**. With only $5$ lines open, how likely is it that they are not enough?

Doing it honestly involves numbers like $\binom{1000}{k}$, frighteningly large. Yet people noticed long ago that whenever you count how often a **rare event** happens (misprints on a page, calls in an hour, photons hitting a detector in a second), the distribution has nearly the same shape, and it depends only on the average count. Why?

To answer that, we first have to turn "the outcome of an experiment" into "a number". That is what a **random variable** does.
</Problem>

<Conjecture>
The larger $n$ and the smaller the success probability $p=\lambda/n$, the closer the bars come to the dots, until only $\lambda$ seems to matter; the plot below them suggests the gap shrinks roughly like $1/n$. The dots follow $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$, which suggests
$$\binom nk\Bigl(\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^k\Bigl(1-\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^{n-k}\ \longrightarrow\ e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\qquad(n\to\infty).$$
Before we can say what it means for one distribution to approach another, we must say what "a number" and "a distribution" are.
</Conjecture>

## Definitions

<Definition title="Random variable">
Let $(\Omega,P)$ be a [probability space](/en/concepts/probability-space). A function $X:\Omega\to\mathbb R$ is called a **random variable**. If $X$ takes only finitely or countably many values, it is **discrete**.
</Definition>

A random variable is neither random nor a variable: it is a perfectly definite function, and all the randomness lives in the outcome $\omega$. In the experiment, the row of cells is one outcome $\omega$ (each of the $n$ trials a success or a failure), and $X(\omega)$ is the number of red cells. For every real $x$, $\{X=x\}=\{\omega\in\Omega:\ X(\omega)=x\}$ is an event, so we may ask for its probability.

<Definition title="Probability mass function">
The **probability mass function** of a discrete random variable $X$ is the function $x\mapsto P(X=x)$, where $x$ ranges over the possible values of $X$.
</Definition>

Its values are non-negative and add up to $1$: the events $\{X=x\}$ for different $x$ are pairwise disjoint and together make up $\Omega$, so countable additivity gives $\sum_xP(X=x)=P(\Omega)=1$. Conversely, as long as we only ask about probabilities involving $X$, the mass function is all we use; what $\Omega$ looks like no longer matters. That is why two distributions can be compared on their own, away from any particular experiment, and the experiment compares exactly two mass functions.

To write down the distribution of a number of successes, we extend [independence](/en/concepts/conditional-probability) from two events to many: $A_1,\dots,A_n$ are **mutually independent** if for every choice of some of them, the probability that all the chosen ones occur equals the product of their probabilities.

<Definition title="Binomial distribution">
Run $n$ trials whose successes are mutually independent, each succeeding with probability $p$. The number of successes $X$ has the **binomial distribution**, written $X\sim B(n,p)$.
</Definition>

<Proposition title="The binomial mass function">
If $X\sim B(n,p)$, then
$$P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,n.$$
</Proposition>

<Proof>
Take one particular outcome: successes exactly at trials $i_1,\dots,i_k$ and failures at the other $n-k$. By independence (replacing some independent events by their complements keeps them independent), its probability is $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, whichever trials the successes fall on. Different outcomes are disjoint, and there are $\binom nk$ ways to choose which $k$ trials succeed; add them up. By the binomial theorem $\sum_k\binom nkp^k(1-p)^{n-k}=(p+1-p)^n=1$, which is where the name comes from.
</Proof>

Where is the tallest bar? Compare neighbouring terms.

<Proposition title="The most likely value">
Let $0<p<1$ and $X\sim B(n,p)$. Then $P(X=k)$ is largest at $k=\lfloor(n+1)p\rfloor$; if $(n+1)p$ is a whole number, $k=(n+1)p$ and $k=(n+1)p-1$ are tied for the maximum.
</Proposition>

<Proof>
For $1\le k\le n$, since $\binom nk\big/\binom n{k-1}=\frac{n-k+1}k$,
$$\frac{P(X=k)}{P(X=k-1)}=\frac{(n-k+1)\,p}{k\,(1-p)}.$$
The ratio exceeds $1$ exactly when $(n-k+1)p>k(1-p)$, that is, when $k<(n+1)p$. So the mass function rises while $k<(n+1)p$, falls once $k>(n+1)p$, and two neighbours are equal where $k=(n+1)p$. Write $m=(n+1)p\in(0,n+1)$. If $m$ is not a whole number, the peak is at $\lfloor m\rfloor$; if it is, then $1\le m\le n$ and $m-1$ and $m$ share the peak.
</Proof>

Since $(n+1)p=np+p$, the most likely value is within $1$ of $np$, but it need not equal $np$; indeed $np$ need not be a whole number, while $X$ only takes whole values. The small triangle in the experiment marks $(n+1)p$, and "tied peak" shows two equally tall bars.

<Definition title="Poisson distribution">
Let $\lambda>0$. A random variable $Y$ with values $0,1,2,\dots$ and
$$P(Y=k)=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!},\qquad k=0,1,2,\dots,$$
has the **Poisson distribution** with parameter $\lambda$, written $Y\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$.
</Definition>

It is a genuine mass function: by the [Taylor expansion](/en/concepts/taylor-series) of $e^\lambda$, $\sum_{k\ge0}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}=e^{-\lambda}e^{\lambda}=1$. Comparing neighbours again, $P(Y=k)/P(Y=k-1)=\lambda/k$, so the peak is at $\lfloor\lambda\rfloor$, shared by $\lambda-1$ and $\lambda$ when $\lambda$ is a whole number.

## Theorems and proofs

<Theorem title="Poisson limit theorem">
Let $\lambda>0$ and $p_n=\lambda/n$ (for $n\ge\lambda$). For each fixed $k=0,1,2,\dots$,
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\binom nk p_n^k(1-p_n)^{n-k}=e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}.$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
Split the expression into three factors:
$$\binom nk\Bigl(\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^k\Bigl(1-\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^{n-k}=\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}\cdot\prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\Bigl(1-\frac jn\Bigr)\cdot\Bigl(1-\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^{n}\Bigl(1-\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^{-k},$$
using $\binom nk\frac1{n^k}=\frac1{k!}\cdot\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)}{n^k}$. With $k$ fixed the product has only $k$ factors, each tending to $1$, so it tends to $1$; likewise $(1-\lambda/n)^{-k}\to1$. Take the logarithm of what remains:
$$\ln\Bigl(1-\frac\lambda n\Bigr)^n=-\lambda\cdot\frac{\ln(1-\lambda/n)}{-\lambda/n}\ \longrightarrow\ -\lambda,$$
because $\frac{\ln(1+x)}{x}\to1$ as $x\to0$, which is just the [derivative](/en/concepts/derivative) of $\ln$ at $1$. Since $e^x$ is continuous, $(1-\lambda/n)^n\to e^{-\lambda}$. Multiplying the three limits gives the result.
</Proof>

Every step uses "$k$ fixed": if $k$ grew with $n$, $\prod(1-j/n)$ need not tend to $1$. So the theorem says that each bar converges separately. The $d$ below the experiment measures the gap between the whole mass functions, and there is a stronger, more useful version for it.

<Remark>
The quantity in the experiment,
$$d=\frac12\sum_k\bigl|P(X=k)-P(Y=k)\bigr|,$$
is the **total variation distance** between the two distributions. One can show that it equals the largest possible difference between $P(X\in A)$ and $P(Y\in A)$ over all sets $A$. In other words, if you answer **any** question about the count with the Poisson law instead of the binomial one, you are off by at most $d$. In 1960 Le Cam proved
$$d\le np^2=\frac{\lambda^2}{n},$$
and the trials need not share one success probability: with probabilities $p_i$, still $d\le\sum_ip_i^2$. On the log–log plot this bound is a line of slope $-1$ (the dashed line in the experiment), and the true $d$ runs parallel to it: ten times as many trials, a tenth of the error.
</Remark>

<Warning>
**Large $n$ alone is not enough; $p$ must be small.** Keep $n=20$: with $\lambda=1$ ($p=0.05$) the distance is about $0.014$, with $\lambda=10$ ($p=0.5$) about $0.17$. $B(20,\tfrac12)$ can never exceed $20$ and is much narrower than $\mathrm{Poisson}(10)$. The next section shows that the binomial variance is $np(1-p)$ and the Poisson variance is $\lambda=np$: they differ by the factor $1-p$. The Poisson approximation is about **rare events**: each unlikely on its own, but with many chances.
</Warning>

<Warning>
**Same distribution does not mean equal.** Toss a fair coin $10$ times. The number of heads $X$ and the number of tails $10-X$ both follow $B(10,\tfrac12)$, exactly the same distribution, yet unless the split is exactly $5$ to $5$ they are never equal. A distribution records the probability of each value, not which $\omega$ produced it. Likewise $X=3$ is not a number but an event, $\{\omega:X(\omega)=3\}$.
</Warning>

## Applications

<Application title="Back to the start: how many lines">
The number of calls is $X\sim B(1000,\,0.003)$, with $\lambda=np=3$. With the Poisson law,
$$P(X\ge6)=1-\sum_{k=0}^{5}e^{-3}\frac{3^k}{k!}\approx0.084,$$
against the exact $0.0836$. With only $5$ lines, roughly one hour in twelve runs short; with $7$, $P(X\ge8)\approx0.012$, about one hour in eighty. Neither $1000$ nor $0.003$ appears in the calculation, only their product $3$: double the customers and halve each one's chance of calling, and the answer barely moves.
</Application>

<Application title="The birthday problem once more">
$n$ people form $\binom n2$ pairs, and each pair shares a birthday with probability $\frac1{365}$. The number of pairs that share is a count of many rare events, approximately $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$ with $\lambda=\binom n2/365$. Hence
$$P(\text{no shared birthday})\approx e^{-\lambda}=\exp\Bigl(-\frac{n(n-1)}{730}\Bigr),$$
exactly the exponential that the [probability spaces](/en/concepts/probability-space) section obtained from $1-x\le e^{-x}$. At $n=23$, $\lambda=\frac{253}{365}\approx0.693\approx\ln2$: the approximation gives $0.500$, the exact value is $0.493$. The pairs are not quite independent (if A and B share a day and B and C do, then so do A and C), but the dependence is weak. "The number of many nearly independent rare events is approximately Poisson" is a principle far wider than this section's theorem.
</Application>

<Application title="Missed photons: total probability as a sum over a distribution">
The number of photons reaching a detector in one second is $N\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda)$, and each photon is recorded with probability $q$, independently. What is the distribution of the number recorded, $M$? Split by the value of $N$. This is the [law of total probability](/en/concepts/conditional-probability), with the sum now running over a distribution:
$$P(M=m)=\sum_{n\ge m}\binom nm q^m(1-q)^{n-m}\cdot e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^n}{n!}=e^{-\lambda}\frac{(\lambda q)^m}{m!}\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{\bigl(\lambda(1-q)\bigr)^j}{j!}=e^{-\lambda q}\frac{(\lambda q)^m}{m!}.$$
(The second step puts $j=n-m$.) So $M\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda q)$: losing a random share of a Poisson count leaves a Poisson count with a discounted mean. Asking the reverse question with Bayes' theorem, "given $m$ recorded, how many arrived?", gives $P(N=n\mid M=m)=e^{-\lambda(1-q)}\dfrac{(\lambda(1-q))^{n-m}}{(n-m)!}$: the number missed, $N-m$, is $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda(1-q))$ **whatever** $m$ is.
</Application>

## Further: waiting times and densities

The same idea answers another question: **how long until the next call?** Let calls arrive at an average rate of $\lambda$ per hour. Cut $[0,t]$ into $n$ short pieces; each contains a call with probability about $\lambda t/n$, so by the Poisson limit theorem the number of calls in $[0,t]$ is approximately $\mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda t)$. The waiting time $T$ exceeds $t$ exactly when no call arrives in $[0,t]$:
$$P(T>t)=e^{-\lambda t},\qquad t\ge0.$$
$T$ can be any real number in $[0,\infty)$, and $P(T=t)=0$ for every particular $t$, so a mass function is useless here. We need another description.

<Definition title="Distribution function and density">
The **distribution function** of a random variable $X$ is $F(x)=P(X\le x)$. If there is a non-negative function $f$ such that for every real $x$
$$F(x)=\int_{-\infty}^xf(t)\,dt,$$
then $X$ is called **continuous** and $f$ is its **probability density**.
</Definition>

Then $P(a<X\le b)=F(b)-F(a)=\int_a^bf(t)\,dt$: probability is area under the density curve. Where $f$ is continuous, the [fundamental theorem of calculus](/en/concepts/integral) gives $F'(x)=f(x)$. The waiting time has distribution function $F(t)=1-e^{-\lambda t}$; differentiating gives the density $f(t)=\lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t\ge0$, the **exponential distribution**. A density is not a probability and can exceed $1$; it is $f(x)\,\Delta x$ that approximates the probability of landing in $(x,x+\Delta x]$. Draw $a\exp(-ax)$ in the plotter and drag the parameter $a$: the larger $a$, the taller and narrower the curve, and the area under it is always $1$. The $p(w\mid D)$ in the previous section's Bayesian neural networks is a density of exactly this kind.

<Remark>
The [probability spaces](/en/concepts/probability-space) section assumed $\Omega$ finite or countable, where every subset can be an event. A random variable with continuous values such as $T$ needs an uncountable $\Omega$, and then not every subset can sensibly be given a probability: $P$ is defined only on a large enough family of sets, one that contains all intervals. The rigorous treatment is measure theory, which this path does not go into; this section only needs probabilities of intervals, where the issue never arises.
</Remark>

<Remark>
The binomial peak sits near $np$, the Poisson peak near $\lambda$. The precise version of this "centre" is the **mean**, and of the "width" the **variance**: the subject of the next section, **mean and variance** (in progress). There we will also see that the covariance of two random variables is an [inner product](/en/concepts/inner-product), so the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives $|\rho|\le1$ at once.
</Remark>
