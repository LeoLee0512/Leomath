## Start from a problem

<Problem>
A car covers 200 km in two hours: average speed 100 km/h. Can you assert that **at some instant the speedometer read exactly 100**?

Intuition says yes: slower than 100 the whole way and you never reach 200 km; faster the whole way and you overshoot. Speed varies continuously, so it must pass through 100. But "intuition says yes" is not a proof. We need a theorem that connects "overall average" to "some single instant" precisely.
</Problem>

<Observe>
Fix the point $x_0$ below and make $h$ large: the secant joins two endpoints. The mean value theorem says the tangent somewhere in between is parallel to that secant. Try a few functions and hunt for that place.

<Experiment slug="secant-tangent" />
</Observe>

<Conjecture>
If $f$ is "nice enough" on $[a,b]$ (continuous, differentiable), there is $\xi\in(a,b)$ with
$$f'(\xi)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$
Strategy for the proof: **reduce to the special case of equal endpoint values**, where all we need is an extremum.
</Conjecture>

## Definition

"All we need is an extremum" requires saying what an extremum is. Here it is compared only with nearby values, not with the whole interval.

<Definition title="Local extrema and stationary points">
Let $f$ be defined on a neighbourhood of $x_0$. If there is $\delta>0$ with $f(x)\le f(x_0)$ whenever $|x-x_0|<\delta$, then $x_0$ is a **local maximum** of $f$; with $\ge$ instead of $\le$ it is a **local minimum**. Both are called **extrema**. If $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$ and $f'(x_0)=0$, then $x_0$ is a **stationary point** of $f$.
</Definition>

The definition needs points of the domain on both sides of $x_0$, so the endpoints of an interval are never extrema in this sense. That matters in the proof below.

<Lemma title="Fermat's lemma">
If $x_0$ is an extremum of $f$ and $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, then $f'(x_0)=0$.
</Lemma>

<Proof>
Say $x_0$ is a local maximum (for a minimum apply the same argument to $-f$). For $0<|h|<\delta$ we have $f(x_0+h)\le f(x_0)$, so
$$\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}\le0\ (h>0),\qquad\frac{f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)}{h}\ge0\ (h<0).$$
Since $f$ is differentiable at $x_0$, both one-sided limits equal $f'(x_0)$; hence $f'(x_0)\le0$ and $f'(x_0)\ge0$, that is, $f'(x_0)=0$.
</Proof>

<Warning>
Fermat's lemma does not run backwards: **a stationary point need not be an extremum.** $f(x)=x^3$ has derivative $0$ at $0$, yet it is below $0$ on the left and above on the right, so $0$ is no extremum. Also, an extremum may have no derivative at all, like $|x|$ at $0$. The lemma only says that a differentiable extremum is a stationary point.
</Warning>

## Theorems and proofs

<Theorem title="Rolle's theorem">
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, with $f(a)=f(b)$. Then there is $\xi\in(a,b)$ with $f'(\xi)=0$.
</Theorem>

<Proof>
A continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum $M$ and a minimum $m$ (extreme value theorem). If $M=m$, $f$ is constant and any $\xi$ works. Otherwise at least one of $M,m$ differs from $f(a)=f(b)$ and is attained at an interior point $\xi\in(a,b)$; say $f(\xi)=M$. Since $\xi$ is interior, the points near it lie in $[a,b]$ and none has a value above $M$, so $\xi$ is a local maximum. $f$ is differentiable at $\xi$, so $f'(\xi)=0$ by Fermat's lemma.
</Proof>

The heart of this step: **the extreme value lies inside, an interior extreme point is an extremum, and a differentiable extremum is a stationary point.** Equal endpoint values guarantee that the extreme values are not found only at the endpoints.

<Theorem title="Lagrange's mean value theorem">
Let $f$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Then there is $\xi\in(a,b)$ with
$$f'(\xi)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$
</Theorem>

<Proof>
The secant through the endpoints is
$$\ell(x)=f(a)+\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a),$$
whose derivative is the constant average slope $m=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}$. Put $g(x)=f(x)-\ell(x)$. Then $g$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and since the secant passes through both endpoints, $g(a)=g(b)=0$. Rolle gives $\xi$ with $g'(\xi)=0$, i.e. $f'(\xi)-m=0$.
</Proof>

The chain: build the secant → subtract it → equal endpoints → Rolle → conclusion. The entire trick is "subtract the secant".

## Which hypothesis cannot be dropped

<Example title="Not differentiable: f(x) = |x|">
On $[-1,1]$ the secant slope is $0$, but $f'$ takes only the values $-1$ and $1$, and $f$ is not differentiable at $0$. No $\xi$ satisfies the conclusion. **Differentiability on the open interval** cannot be dropped.
</Example>

<Example title="Discontinuous at an endpoint">
Let $f(x)=x$ for $0\le x<1$ and $f(1)=2$. The secant slope is $2$; the interior derivative is identically $1$. **Continuity at the endpoints** cannot be dropped, even at a single point.
</Example>

<Warning>
$\xi$ **exists**, but the theorem says nothing about where it is, how many there are, or how to find it. Never treat $\xi$ as a quantity you can solve for; the theorem's value lies in estimates: $|f(b)-f(a)|\le\max|f'|\cdot|b-a|$.
</Warning>

## Applications

<Application title="Zero derivative means constant">
If $f'\equiv0$ on an interval $I$, then for any $a<b$ in $I$, $f(b)-f(a)=f'(\xi)(b-a)=0$. This corollary is used in part II of the fundamental theorem in [The integral](/en/concepts/integral) and in the uniqueness proof for $y'=ky$ in [What is an ODE](/en/concepts/what-is-ode). **Without the mean value theorem both proofs are empty.**
</Application>

<Application title="Monotonicity test">
$f'>0$ on $(a,b)$ implies $f$ is strictly increasing: for $x_1<x_2$, $f(x_2)-f(x_1)=f'(\xi)(x_2-x_1)>0$.
</Application>

<Application title="Taylor's theorem">
The Lagrange-remainder proof in [Taylor expansion](/en/concepts/taylor-series) is Rolle's theorem applied $n+1$ times to a carefully built function.
</Application>

<Remark>
The mean value theorem is the bridge from local information to global behaviour: knowing the derivative at every point controls the difference of function values between any two points. Nearly every statement of the form "a property of $f'$ implies a property of $f$" rests on it.
</Remark>
